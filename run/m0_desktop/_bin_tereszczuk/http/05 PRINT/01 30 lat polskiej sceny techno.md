# 30 lat polskiej sceny techno

!(files/print/30_lat.png)

*30 Years of the Polish Techno Scene* was conceived as something much larger than a book about music. It was created as a collective act of self-documentation by a culture that had shaped the lives of thousands of people in Poland for three decades, yet had never been given a sufficiently broad, serious, and internally voiced account. As one of the project’s principal initiators and organizing forces, Radosław Tereszczuk helped bring together not a single storyline, but a whole field of memories, experiences, disagreements, scenes, sounds, and bodies in motion.

From the beginning, the project was non-commercial in spirit and communal in method. It did not emerge as an external study of the scene, nor as a neatly packaged market product, but as a book built from within: by people who had actually made the scene, sustained it, transformed it, and lived through it. Its contributors included promoters, club founders, festival organizers, DJs, producers, label people, journalists, and participants whose role was no less essential simply because it was harder to name. The dancefloor itself was treated as a site of authorship. The scene was made not only by those behind the decks or behind the institutions, but also by those who brought their attention, energy, commitment, and bodies into the shared space of collective experience.

This was crucial, because Polish techno could never be truthfully described through a single voice. It was and remains too complex, too decentralized, too internally diverse, too historically uneven, and too deeply bound to local conditions for that. It unfolded across large cities and smaller towns, in clubs and outside them, in official venues and improvised spaces, in forests, warehouses, beaches, squats, basements, and temporary autonomous zones. It involved different generations, different class backgrounds, different aesthetics, and often incompatible understandings of what techno even was. Any attempt to reduce that multiplicity to one authoritative narrative would have required brutal simplifications and false hierarchies.

For that reason, the book deliberately refused synthesis. It did not privilege one city over another, one scene over another, one stylistic lineage over another, or one ideological reading over another. Instead, it embraced the only form adequate to the subject: an anthology of individual texts written by people who had taken part in shaping the Polish techno scene over thirty years. Each of these voices brought its own memory, emphasis, bias, rhythm, and emotional truth. Some accounts overlap, some complement one another, and some contradict each other in important ways. That lack of uniformity is not a weakness of the book but one of its deepest strengths. It reflects the actual structure of the culture it documents: fragmented, polyphonic, improvised, and alive.

The result is not a closed history, but a living archive. It does not pretend to settle disputes or establish a final canon. It shows how a scene remembers itself when it is finally given the chance to speak in its own voice. Dates, places, lineups, clubs, festivals, labels, private archives, scenes within scenes, local myths, and half-forgotten episodes all matter here, but they matter as parts of something larger: the long social and emotional history of a community held together by sound, movement, repetition, intensity, curiosity, and a shared sense that something important was happening on the margins before it was visible from the center.

That is why the book is intentionally closer to conversation than to academic synthesis. Its form resembles what happens when veterans of club culture step outside for a moment, away from the volume and pressure of the room, and finally begin to hear one another clearly. In that more intimate space, stories emerge: half-legendary, sharply remembered, disputed, affectionate, sometimes chaotic, often funny, sometimes painful. One memory triggers another. One account is corrected by the next. A place long gone suddenly becomes vivid again because someone else remembers its smell, its stairs, the cold outside, the first track of the night, the people who never made it into official histories. This atmosphere of lived recall gives the project its human scale and its unusual honesty.

What makes *30 Years of the Polish Techno Scene* especially important is that it documents techno not only as a musical genre, but as a social world. It presents club culture as a distributed and participatory phenomenon: something generated from below, sustained by communities rather than imposed from above, and inseparable from the people who inhabited it. In that sense, the book is not only about events, records, artists, and institutions. It is also about belonging, about shared attention, about the ways in which underground culture becomes a form of collective life.

Seen from this perspective, the project becomes more than an editorial achievement. It is an act of cultural responsibility. It preserves fragile histories before they disappear, gathers dispersed experiences before they are lost, and resists the flattening effects of nostalgia, marketing, and oversimplified retrospection. It gives Polish techno a record commensurate with its actual richness: not a monument carved from one block, but a many-voiced structure built from memory, conflict, affection, effort, and love for the scene.

For all these reasons, *30 Years of the Polish Techno Scene* stands as a rare kind of book: not an outside description of a milieu, but a self-portrait created by that milieu itself. Its ambition was not to close the story, but to open it as widely as possible. And that openness—messy, generous, unsynthesized, and collective—is precisely what makes it true.
